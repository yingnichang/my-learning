The Volatility Surface

The first three lectures build one continuous argument: empirical market data reject constant-volatility thinking; stochastic and rough volatility explain changing variance and short-time scaling; local volatility matches the vanilla surface; affine forward-variance models then unify classical and rough Heston and make characteristic-function pricing tractable.

Course backbone

Data → model restriction → option surface

Stylized facts → random variance → stochastic volatility → roughness → P vs Q → Heston → Dupire local volatility → forward variance → affine models → characteristic functions

The guiding rule is not to memorize isolated equations. For each one, ask what empirical fact motivates it, what assumption is being made, and what observable quantity it helps explain.

01Power lawsTail probability versus densityCore concept

Observation

Many financial and economic quantities have tails that decay much more slowly than a Gaussian tail.

Intuition

A power law has no single characteristic scale in the tail. Very large observations remain materially more likely than under an exponential or Gaussian decay.

Mathematics

Tail law\[P(X>x)\sim Cx^{-\zeta}.\]

If the tail probability behaves like (x^{-\zeta}), then the density behaves like (x^{-(\zeta+1)}).

Worked derivation
  1. Start from (P(X>x)\approx Cx^{-\zeta}).
  2. The density is (f(x)=-\frac{d}{dx}P(X>x)).
  3. Differentiate: (-\frac{d}{dx}(Cx^{-\zeta})=C\zeta x^{-(\zeta+1)}).
  4. So the tail exponent is (zeta), while the density exponent is (zeta+1).

Modeling consequence

Always check what is plotted. A log-log slope near (-3) for a tail probability corresponds to roughly (-4) for the density.

RememberTail exponent and density exponent differ by one.
Common trapReading a histogram slope as if it were a survival-function slope.
02Power-law algebraHow exponents transformWorked rules

Intuition

The fattest tail dominates sums and maxima. Transformations such as (Y=X^\alpha) rescale the exponent.

Mathematics

\[\zeta_{X_1+\cdots+X_n}=\min_i\zeta_i,\qquad \zeta_{\max(X_i)}=\min_i\zeta_i.\]
\[Y=X^\alpha\quad\Rightarrow\quad \zeta_Y=\frac{\zeta_X}{\alpha}.\]
Worked derivation of (Y=X^\alpha)
  1. (P(Y>y)=P(X^\alpha>y)).
  2. For (alpha>0), this is (P(X>y^{1/\alpha})).
  3. Use the tail law: (P(X>x)\sim x^{-\zeta_X}).
  4. Then (P(Y>y)\sim (y^{1/\alpha})^{-\zeta_X}=y^{-\zeta_X/\alpha}).
  5. Therefore (zeta_Y=\zeta_X/\alpha).

Modeling consequence

This algebra lets one connect power laws across trade size, market impact, firm size, and returns without refitting every relation from scratch.

03Pareto / ZipfWhy 80/20 implies an exponent near oneWorked calculation

Observation

Income, wealth, city size, and firm size often display extreme concentration.

Mathematics

\[W=P^{1-1/\alpha}.\]
Worked 80/20 calculation
  1. Set (P=0.2) and (W=0.8).
  2. (0.8=0.2^{1-1/\alpha}).
  3. Take logs: (log 0.8=(1-1/\alpha)log 0.2).
  4. Divide: (1-1/\alpha=\log(0.8)/\log(0.2)\approx0.13865).
  5. So (1/\alpha\approx0.86135).
  6. (alpha\approx1/0.86135\approx1.161).

Modeling consequence

An exponent only modestly above one means an extremely fat tail and severe concentration.

04ReturnsLog returns are not GaussianEmpirical foundation

Observation

SPX daily log returns have a sharper center and much fatter tails than a normal distribution.

Mathematics

\[r_t=\log\frac{S_t}{S_{t-1}}.\]
\[P(|r|>x)\sim x^{-3}\quad\Rightarrow\quad f(r)\sim |r|^{-4}.\]

Intuition

A Student-(t) with about three degrees of freedom mimics this empirical tail behavior much better than a Gaussian.

Modeling consequence

A constant-volatility Gaussian model cannot reproduce the empirical frequency of extreme daily moves.

05Tail truncationWhy a financial power law cannot continue foreverConceptual proof

Intuition

Stock prices are exponentials of log returns. If positive log-return tails stayed power-law forever, exponential moments could blow up.

Mathematics

\[S=e^X,\qquad E[S]=E[e^X].\]

A polynomially decaying positive tail is not enough to control the exponential factor (e^X) at infinity.

Modeling consequence

Interpret empirical “power law” as scaling over a substantial range, not as a literal law from zero to infinity.

06Stylized factsReturns are hard to predict; volatility is persistentCore intuition

Observation

Raw returns have little serial correlation, while (|r_t|) and (r_t^2) exhibit persistent autocorrelation.

Intuition

The sign of tomorrow’s return is difficult to forecast, but the scale of tomorrow’s move is much more predictable.

Modeling consequence

Volatility itself should be a state variable with persistence. This is the empirical motivation for stochastic volatility.

07Business timeIntegrated variance as a random clockBridge to SV

Intuition

A calendar day can contain very different amounts of “financial time.” Quiet days are short in business time; crisis days are long.

Mathematics

\[\tau(t)=\int_0^t v_s\,ds.\]

Conditionally on a path of variance, Brownian returns accumulate variance according to this random clock.

Modeling consequence

Mixtures of Gaussian returns with different realized variances naturally create a high central peak and fat unconditional tails.

08Conditional GaussianityWhy random variance creates fat tailsKey bridge

Intuition

Inside each volatility regime, returns may look close to Gaussian. Mixing low- and high-volatility regimes makes the unconditional distribution sharply peaked with fat tails.

Mathematics

\[\frac{dS_t}{S_t}=\sqrt{V_t}\,dZ_t.\]

Modeling consequence

Instead of forcing one exotic static return distribution, model a random, persistent variance process.

09Cont stylized factsThe checklist a volatility model should respectReview map

Observation

Key facts include weak return autocorrelation, heavy tails, intermittency, volatility clustering, slow decay of volatility dependence, leverage, conditional heavy tails, and volume-volatility correlation.

Modeling consequence

A model that fits one option smile but destroys these dynamics is descriptively incomplete.

10Leverage effectWhy price declines and volatility rises are linkedSurface connection

Observation

Equity-index price moves and volatility changes are strongly negatively related.

Mathematics

\[\operatorname{Corr}(d\log S_t,d\log V_t)<0.\]

Modeling consequence

Negative spot-vol correlation is a primary source of negative equity implied-volatility skew in stochastic-volatility models.

11Rough volatilityThe Hurst exponent and scalingCore concept

Observation

Log realized variance increments display power-law scaling across time horizons.

Mathematics

\[E\left[|\log V_{t+\Delta}-\log V_t|^q\right]\propto \Delta^{qH}.\]

Intuition

(H=1/2) is Brownian roughness. Empirical estimates well below (1/2) imply even more jagged volatility paths.

How (H) is estimated
  1. Choose moments (q).
  2. For each lag (Delta), compute the empirical (q)-th absolute moment of log-vol increments.
  3. Take logs: (log m_q(\Delta)=a_q+\zeta_q\log\Delta).
  4. The slope gives (zeta_q).
  5. Under monofractal scaling, (zeta_q=qH), so regress (zeta_q) on (q) through the origin to estimate (H).

Modeling consequence

Conventional smooth Markovian volatility diffusions miss this short-scale roughness.

12Volatility surfaceFrom flat Black–Scholes vol to market smilesOption data

Observation

Equity implied volatility depends on strike and maturity. Lower strikes usually trade at higher implied volatility.

Mathematics

\[k=\log(K/F),\qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{imp}}=\sigma_{\mathrm{imp}}(k,T).\]

Modeling consequence

A useful model must fit not only one volatility level but the cross-sectional shape and its dynamics.

13Short-dated skewPower-law explosion of ATM skewEmpirical restriction

Mathematics

\[\text{ATM Skew}(T)=\left.\frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{imp}}(k,T)}{\partial k}\right|_{k=0},\qquad |\text{Skew}(T)|\sim T^{-\gamma}.\]

Intuition

Short expiries react most violently to near-term downside risk, so the skew steepens as maturity shrinks.

Modeling consequence

The observed short-time exponent is a direct restriction on admissible volatility dynamics and is one of the main empirical arguments for rough volatility.

14Surface dynamicsSkew-stickiness ratioDynamics test

Observation

ATM implied volatility co-moves with spot in a fairly stable way across expiries on a given day.

Mathematics

\[R(T)=\frac{\beta(T)}{\psi(T)}.\]

Here (psi(T)) is ATM strike skew and (eta(T)) is the spot/ATM-vol regression coefficient in quadratic-covariation form.

Intuition

Sticky delta gives (R=0); sticky strike gives (R=1). Empirical values are neither extreme and are roughly expiry-independent on a given day.

Modeling consequence

Matching a static smile is insufficient: the model must also reproduce how that smile moves when spot moves.

15Power lawsZipf's law as rank-size scalingpp. 1–2

Observation

City sizes, firm sizes, wealth, and word frequencies often line up approximately as straight lines on log-log rank plots.

Intuition

Rank is another representation of a tail distribution. When the tail exponent is near one, size is approximately inversely proportional to rank.

Mathematics

Pr(S > x) ~ x−ζ; if ζ ≈ 1, then ranked size S(r) is approximately proportional to 1/r.

Trading / modeling consequence

A rank-size plot is a fast empirical diagnostic for scale-free behavior, but only over the range where the log-log relation is stable.

Common confusionZipf is not a separate idea from Pareto here; it is a rank representation of the same heavy-tail structure.
Memory ruleTail exponent near 1 → rank-size slope near −1.
16Power-law algebraSums, maxima, minima, and tail dominancep. 2

Observation

The lecture lists exponent rules for combinations of independent heavy-tailed variables.

Intuition

For sums and maxima, the fattest tail dominates. For a minimum, every variable must be large at the same time, so tail probabilities multiply and exponents add.

Mathematics

ζ(X₁+⋯+Xₙ) = ζ(max Xᵢ) = minᵢ ζᵢ; ζ(min Xᵢ) = Σᵢ ζᵢ.
Worked steps
  1. For a maximum, Pr(max Xᵢ > x) = 1 − ∏ᵢ[1 − Pr(Xᵢ > x)].
  2. For rare tail events, the leading contribution comes from the slowest-decaying component, so the smallest exponent dominates.
  3. For a minimum under independence, Pr(min Xᵢ > x) = ∏ᵢ Pr(Xᵢ > x) ~ x−Σζᵢ.

Trading / modeling consequence

These rules explain why one very heavy-tailed component can dominate an aggregate.

Common confusionThe formulas require the lecture's heavy-tail assumptions; they are not universal identities for arbitrary distributions.
Memory ruleSum/max inherit the fattest tail; min adds exponents.
17Return tailsStudent-t, the cubic law, and finite momentspp. 7–12

Observation

Daily SPX returns fit a Student-t far better than a Gaussian in the center and tails, with a tail exponent near three over a substantial empirical range.

Intuition

A Student-t can create both the sharp center and the frequent large moves that a Gaussian misses.

Mathematics

For Student-t with ν degrees of freedom: density p(x) ~ |x|−(ν+1), while tail probability Pr(|X| > x) ~ x−ν. Variance = ν/(ν−2) for ν > 2.
Worked steps
  1. If ν ≈ 3, the survival-tail exponent is approximately 3.
  2. The corresponding density exponent is approximately 4.
  3. This is exactly the one-unit difference between tail and density exponents from Lesson 1.

Trading / modeling consequence

Gaussian risk models can materially understate the empirical frequency of large daily moves.

Common confusionThe cubic law refers to an approximate empirical tail regime, not an exact law extending to infinity.
Memory ruleStudent-t ν≈3 ↔ cubic survival tail.
18AggregationWhy weekly and monthly returns look less extremepp. 13–14

Observation

Weekly and monthly returns remain fat-tailed, but aggregation pushes their shapes somewhat closer to Gaussian behavior.

Intuition

Adding many shocks averages some idiosyncratic detail, while persistent volatility prevents rapid convergence to a simple Gaussian.

Mathematics

R(t,t+m) = r(t+1) + r(t+2) + ⋯ + r(t+m).

Trading / modeling consequence

Model validation should check several horizons. A model that fits daily tails but fails weekly or monthly aggregation misses dependence structure.

Common confusionCloser to Gaussian does not mean Gaussian; volatility clustering slows the averaging effect.
Memory ruleAggregation softens tails; dependence keeps them alive.
19Diffusion densitiesFrom an SDE to the Fokker-Planck equationpp. 14–15

Observation

The lecture derives how the probability density of a diffusion evolves through time.

Intuition

Itô describes functions of individual random paths; Fokker-Planck is the corresponding evolution equation for the entire density.

Mathematics

dXₜ = μ(Xₜ,t)dt + σ(Xₜ,t)dWₜ ⇒ ∂ₜp = −∂ₓ(μp) + ½∂ₓₓ(σ²p).
Worked steps
  1. Apply Itô's formula to a smooth test function f(Xₜ,t).
  2. Take expectations; the stochastic dW term has zero expectation.
  3. Write E[f(Xₜ,t)] as an integral of f against the density p.
  4. Use integration by parts to move derivatives from f onto p.
  5. Because the identity holds for suitable f, identify the PDE for p.

Trading / modeling consequence

This is the bridge between path dynamics and stationary tail distributions.

Common confusionThe SDE evolves sample paths; Fokker-Planck evolves their density.
Memory ruleSDE for paths ↔ Fokker-Planck for density.
20Power-law mechanismDeriving the Gabaix tail exponentpp. 15–16

Observation

A proportional-growth diffusion can generate a stationary power-law size distribution once a lower cutoff is imposed.

Intuition

Multiplicative growth is scale free: a one-percent shock has the same proportional meaning for a small or large object.

Mathematics

dXₜ = μXₜdt + νXₜdZₜ; f(x) ∝ x−(ζ+1); ζ = 1 − 2μ/ν²; and ζ = 1/(1 − Smin/S̄).
Worked steps
  1. Set the stationary Fokker-Planck time derivative to zero.
  2. Insert the candidate density f(x)=C x−(ζ+1) and match powers of x.
  3. The non-degenerate solution gives ζ = 1 − 2μ/ν².
  4. Normalize the Pareto density above Smin and compute the mean S̄ = Smin · ζ/(ζ−1).
  5. Multiply through: S̄(ζ−1)=Smin·ζ.
  6. Rearrange: ζ(S̄−Smin)=S̄.
  7. Divide by S̄−Smin: ζ = S̄/(S̄−Smin) = 1/(1−Smin/S̄).
  8. When Smin is much smaller than S̄, Smin/S̄ is close to zero, so ζ ≈ 1.

Trading / modeling consequence

When the mean scale is much larger than the minimum scale, a Zipf-like exponent near one emerges.

Common confusionThe approximation ζ≈1 comes from scale separation; it does not require setting Smin exactly to zero.
Memory ruleLarge mean relative to minimum → ζ near 1.
21Business timeOxford-Man realized variance and normalized returnspp. 16–18

Observation

High-frequency data provide daily estimates of integrated variance that can be used to normalize returns.

Intuition

Calendar time treats a quiet day and a crisis day as equal lengths; variance time stretches the crisis day and compresses the quiet day.

Mathematics

Financial clock τ(t) = ∫₀ᵗ Vₛ ds. Returns divided by the square root of integrated variance are empirically much closer to Gaussian.

Trading / modeling consequence

This motivates stochastic volatility as a random time change rather than a static exotic return distribution.

Common confusionVariance-normalized returns are closer to Gaussian, not perfectly Gaussian.
Memory ruleVariance is the market's clock.
22High frequencyTrading time versus clock timepp. 18–21

Observation

At intraday horizons, returns indexed by numbers of trades can behave differently from returns indexed by elapsed clock time.

Intuition

Trading activity is itself random, so a fixed minute can contain very different amounts of information and risk.

Mathematics

Calendar-time return = price change over a random amount of trading activity.

Trading / modeling consequence

Execution and microstructure models often benefit from activity clocks, volume time, or variance time.

Common confusionTrading time does not magically make returns exactly normal; it isolates one source of heteroskedasticity.
Memory ruleRandom activity makes calendar time heteroskedastic.
23Market microstructureOrder-size tails and market impactpp. 21–22

Observation

Market order sizes are themselves heavy-tailed; the lecture connects this fact to proposed explanations for return tails.

Intuition

If trade sizes have fat tails and price impact is nonlinear, scale-free order-flow variation can transmit into returns.

Mathematics

If Pr(Q>x) ~ x−ζQ and price impact Δp ~ Qδ, then the transformation rule suggests ζ(Δp) ≈ ζQ/δ.

Trading / modeling consequence

This gives a structural route from trading activity to return tails when evaluating microstructure explanations.

Common confusionThe relation is a mechanism hypothesis, not proof that order size alone explains volatility.
Memory ruleHeavy-tailed flow + impact can create heavy-tailed returns.
24Stylized factsZero return autocorrelation versus persistent volatilitypp. 22–27

Observation

Raw returns show little linear autocorrelation at daily horizons, while absolute and squared returns show strong positive autocorrelation.

Intuition

The sign of tomorrow's move is hard to predict, but the magnitude of tomorrow's move is much more predictable.

Mathematics

Corr(rₜ,rₜ₊ₖ) ≈ 0, while Corr(|rₜ|,|rₜ₊ₖ|) > 0 and Corr(rₜ²,rₜ₊ₖ²) > 0.

Trading / modeling consequence

Volatility forecasting can be useful even when directional return forecasting is weak.

Common confusionVolatility clustering concerns magnitude, not repeated positive or negative signs.
Memory ruleDirection forgets; magnitude remembers.
25LeverageNegative returns and future volatilitypp. 27–31

Observation

Negative equity returns tend to be followed by higher volatility more strongly than positive returns of similar magnitude.

Intuition

A price drop raises financial leverage and often coincides with risk-off demand, both pushing volatility upward.

Mathematics

Corr(rₜ, Vₜ₊Δ) < 0 for equities over relevant horizons.

Stochastic-volatility models typically use negative spot-vol correlation to reproduce equity skew dynamics.

Common confusionThe leverage effect is a time-series relation; implied-volatility skew is a cross-strike option-price object.

Observation

The same SPX level can coexist with very different VIX levels depending on the path and market state that led there.

Intuition

Volatility contains state information that current spot alone does not summarize.

Mathematics

In general, VIXₜ ≠ f(Sₜ) for any one-dimensional state function f.

Implied volatility varies across both strike and maturity, forming a two-dimensional surface rather than one constant volatility number.

15Stochastic volatilityWhy go beyond Black–Scholes?Motivation

Observation

Volatility fluctuates through time and vanilla option prices imply smiles, not a flat volatility.

Intuition

Black–Scholes can still be a quoting formula, but its single-volatility world is not a realistic joint model for spot and option dynamics.

Mathematics

\[\frac{dS_t}{S_t}=\sqrt{V_t}\,dZ_t,\qquad V_t=\frac{d}{dt}\langle\log S\rangle_t.\]

Modeling consequence

SV models can generate realistic smiles while preserving arbitrage-free dynamics, making them more coherent for exotic-option pricing and hedging.

16Mixture intuitionWhy random volatility creates fat tailsToy model

Intuition

Low-volatility regimes create the sharp center; high-volatility regimes create the fat tails. Mixing them reproduces the stylized shape of return histograms.

Mathematics

\[f(x)=\tfrac12\phi(x;0,0.1^2)+\tfrac12\phi(x;0,0.3^2).\]

Modeling consequence

Variance should be random and persistent, not a one-off jump variable.

17Variance dynamicsWhy variance should be stationary in the long runEconomic argument

Intuition

If volatility had no long-run restoring force, the distribution of a large company’s volatility decades ahead would drift to absurdly tiny or huge values. Mean reversion keeps long-run variance economically plausible.

Modeling consequence

Classical models such as Heston build stationarity through mean-reverting variance dynamics.

18Fractional Brownian motionHow (H) changes dependence and roughnessRough-vol basis

Mathematics

\[E[W_t^H W_s^H]=\tfrac12\big(|t|^{2H}+|s|^{2H}-|t-s|^{2H}\big).\]

Intuition

(H=1/2) gives Brownian motion; (H>1/2) gives positively correlated increments; (H<1/2) gives negatively correlated increments and rougher paths.

Modeling consequence

Rough-vol models use fractional-type behavior for volatility, not for the tradable stock price itself.

19Measures(P) versus (Q): forecasting versus pricingEssential distinction

Intuition

(P) describes real-world statistical evolution; (Q) is the pricing measure under which discounted tradable asset prices are martingales.

Mathematics

Under (P)\[\frac{dS_t}{S_t}=\mu_t\,dt+\sqrt{V_t}\,dZ_t^P.\]
Under (Q), with zero rates\[\frac{dS_t}{S_t}=\sqrt{V_t}\,dZ_t^Q.\]

Modeling consequence

Historical volatility forecasting is a (P)-problem; vanilla and exotic option pricing is primarily a (Q)-problem.

20Risk premiumWhy the equity risk premium can be proportional to varianceWorked derivation

Mathematics

With fraction (pi_t) of wealth in stock,

\[\frac{dW_t}{W_t}=\pi_t\mu_tdt+\pi_t\sqrt{V_t}\,dZ_t^P.\]
Worked log-utility optimization
  1. Apply Itô to (log W_t): [d\log W_t=(\pi_t\mu_t-\tfrac12\pi_t^2V_t)dt+\pi_t\sqrt{V_t}dZ_t^P.]
  2. The instantaneous expected growth rate is (g(\pi)=\pi\mu-\tfrac12\pi^2V).
  3. Differentiate: (g'(\pi)=\mu-\pi V).
  4. Set to zero: (pi^*=\mu/V).
  5. If the optimal share (pi^*=1/\beta) is constant, then (mu=\beta V).

Modeling consequence

This motivates a Girsanov shift proportional to (sqrt V), linking (P)- and (Q)-dynamics.

21Change of measureGirsanov shift from (P) to (Q)Worked substitution

Mathematics

\[dZ_t^Q=dZ_t^P+\beta\sqrt{V_t}\,dt.\]
Why the drift disappears
  1. Start under (P): (dS/S=\beta Vdt+\sqrt V,dZ^P).
  2. Rearrange the measure change: (dZ^P=dZ^Q-\beta\sqrt Vdt).
  3. Substitute: (dS/S=\beta Vdt+\sqrt V(dZ^Q-\beta\sqrt Vdt)).
  4. The drift terms cancel: (eta Vdt-\beta Vdt=0).
  5. Hence (dS/S=\sqrt V,dZ^Q), making (S) a (Q)-martingale when rates are zero.
22Classical HestonMean-reverting square-root varianceCanonical SV model

Mathematics

\[dV_t=-\lambda(V_t-\bar V)dt+\eta\sqrt{V_t}\,dW_t,\qquad d\langle W,Z\rangle_t=\rho,dt.\]

Intuition

(lambda) controls mean-reversion speed, (ar V) the long-run variance, (eta) vol-of-vol, and ( ho) spot-vol correlation. Negative ( ho) creates negative equity skew.

Modeling consequence

Heston is analytically convenient and economically interpretable, but conventional Heston does not reproduce the empirical short-time rough scaling well.

23Heston under (P) and (Q)How the variance parameters changeMeasure effect

Mathematics

Under (P)\[dV_t=-\lambda^P(V_t-\bar V^P)dt+\eta\sqrt{V_t},dW_t^P.\]
Under (Q)\[dV_t=-\lambda^Q(V_t-\bar V^Q)dt+\eta\sqrt{V_t},dW_t^Q.\]
\[\lambda^Q=\lambda^P+\rho\eta,\qquad \lambda^Q\bar V^Q=\lambda^P\bar V^P.\]

Modeling consequence

Option markets reveal (Q)-parameters. Historical estimation reveals (P)-parameters. Their difference is volatility risk compensation.

24Local volatilityWhat local volatility is—and is notPhilosophy

Intuition

Local volatility is the unique one-factor diffusion that reproduces all vanilla option prices across strikes and maturities. It need not be interpreted as the true physical law of volatility.

Modeling consequence

Think of local volatility as an “effective theory” or state-dependent projection of richer stochastic-volatility dynamics.

25Option surfaceRecovering the risk-neutral density from callsKey identity

Mathematics

\[C(K,T)=E^Q[(S_T-K)^+],\qquad \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}=\phi(K,T).\]
Derivative logic
  1. (partial_K(S_T-K)^+=-1_{\{S_T>K\}}).
  2. Thus (partial_K C=-Q(S_T>K)).

19Roughness estimationRegressing ζq on q to estimate Hpp. 12–13

24Pricing PDEDeriving the Black-Scholes-Merton PDE under Qp. 24

Observation

  2. Under Q with zero rates, dSₜ = Sₜ√Vₜ dZₜ, so Eₜ[dSₜ]=0.

  6. Set their coefficient equal to zero because C is a Q-martingale.

04ODE solutionSolving the forward-variance ODE step by stepp. 2

dξₜ(u)=κ(u−t)√Vₜ dWₜ, with a deterministic nonnegative decreasing kernel κ satisfying the required integrability.

Trading / modeling consequence

Classical Heston and rough Heston become two different kernel choices inside one common model class.

Common confusionThe theorem restricts the volatility of forward variance; it does not require the initial forward curve to be flat.
Memory ruleAffine forward variance = deterministic lag kernel × √V shock.
14Transform martingaleWhy Mₜ=Eₜ[e^(zX_T)] is a martingalepp. 6–7

Observation

The conditional transform of a fixed terminal payoff is itself a martingale in information time.

Intuition

This is the same tower-property logic used earlier for forward variance, now applied to an exponential terminal payoff.

Mathematics

Mₜ = Eₜ[e^(zX_T)] = exp{zXₜ + Gₜ}; Gₜ = ∫ₜᵀ ξₜ(s)g(T−s;z)ds.
Worked steps
  1. For s<t, take Eₛ[Mₜ].
  2. Substitute Mₜ=E[e^(zX_T)|Fₜ].
  3. Apply the tower property.
  4. Eₛ[Mₜ]=E[e^(zX_T)|Fₛ]=Mₛ.
  5. Therefore the predictable drift of Mₜ must be zero.

Trading / modeling consequence

That zero-drift restriction determines the Riccati equation for g.

Common confusionA martingale may still have stochastic diffusion terms; zero drift does not mean zero movement.
Memory ruleConditional transform ⇒ martingale ⇒ zero drift.
15Riccati derivationStep 1: dynamics of log spot and Gₜpp. 6–7

Observation

To derive the transform equation, the lecture computes the dynamics of Xₜ=log Sₜ and of the forward-variance integral Gₜ.

Intuition

Both stochastic terms are proportional to √Vₜ, which is why all drift terms can be factored by Vₜ.

Mathematics

dXₜ = −½Vₜdt + √Vₜ dZₜ. dGₜ = −Vₜg(τ;z)dt + (κ⋆g)(τ;z)√Vₜ dWₜ, where τ=T−t.
Worked steps
  1. For Xₜ=log Sₜ, apply Itô to dSₜ/Sₜ=√VₜdZₜ.
  2. The Itô correction gives the −½Vₜdt term.
  3. For Gₜ, differentiate an integral whose lower limit is t.
  4. The moving lower limit contributes −ξₜ(t)g(T−t;z)dt = −Vₜg(τ;z)dt.
  5. Insert dξₜ(s)=κ(s−t)√VₜdWₜ into the remaining integral.
  6. Change variables to recognize the convolution (κ⋆g)(τ;z).

Trading / modeling consequence

This calculation prepares every piece needed to apply Itô to Mₜ.

Common confusionThe minus sign in the G drift comes from the moving lower integration limit.
Memory ruleDifferentiate lower limit + forward-curve shock.
16Riccati derivationStep 2: quadratic variations and drift cancellationp. 7

Observation

Itô's formula for an exponential requires the quadratic-variation and cross-variation terms.

Intuition

Because X and G share correlated Brownian shocks, the cross term is essential and produces the ρ term in the Riccati equation.

Mathematics

d⟨X⟩=Vdt; d⟨G⟩=V(κ⋆g)²dt; d⟨X,G⟩=ρV(κ⋆g)dt.
Worked steps
  1. Write M=e^(zX+G).
  2. Apply Itô: dM/M = z dX + dG + ½z²d⟨X⟩ + ½d⟨G⟩ + z d⟨X,G⟩.
  3. Insert the dynamics from the previous lesson.
  4. Collect all dt terms; each contains a factor Vₜdt.
  5. The drift coefficient is −½z − g + ½z² + ρz(κ⋆g) + ½(κ⋆g)².
  6. Set it equal to zero because M is a martingale.
  7. Rearrange to obtain g(τ;z)=½(z²−z)+ρz(κ⋆g)(τ;z)+½(κ⋆g)(τ;z)².

Trading / modeling consequence

This convolution Riccati equation is the central transform equation for the affine forward-variance class.

Common confusionThe ½(z²−z) term combines the log-price drift and the Itô correction; neither piece alone gives the full term.
Memory ruleApply Itô, collect Vdt, set drift to zero.
17ConvolutionWhat (κ⋆g)(τ) meansp. 7

Observation

The Riccati equation uses a convolution of the memory kernel κ with the transform loading g.

Intuition

A convolution adds contributions from all intermediate lags. The kernel says how a shock at one lag influences the remaining horizon.

Mathematics

(κ⋆g)(τ) = ∫₀^τ κ(τ−s)g(s)ds.
Worked steps
  1. Fix the total lag τ.
  2. For each intermediate lag s between 0 and τ, evaluate g(s).
  3. Weight it by κ(τ−s), the response over the remaining lag.
  4. Integrate those weighted contributions over all s.

Trading / modeling consequence

Convolution notation lets exponential and power-law memory share the same transform framework.

Common confusionConvolution is an integral operation, not ordinary multiplication.
Memory ruleSlide, multiply, integrate over lag.
18Fractional calculusFractional integration as generalized repeated integrationp. 8

Observation

The rough power-law kernel naturally introduces fractional integrals and derivatives.

Intuition

Repeated ordinary integration already produces a power-law kernel. Replacing factorials by Gamma functions lets the integration order become non-integer.

Mathematics

I^α f(t) = [1/Γ(α)]∫₀ᵗ(t−s)^(α−1)f(s)ds; D^α f(t) = d/dt [I^(1−α)f(t)].

Trading / modeling consequence

This notation turns rough-Heston convolution equations into fractional Riccati differential equations.

Common confusionA fractional derivative is a memory operator; it is not simply 'differentiate α times' in the elementary sense.
Memory ruleGamma function lets integration order become fractional.
19Fractional calculusDeriving Cauchy's repeated-integration formulap. 8

Observation

The lecture first derives the ordinary n-fold integration kernel before extending n to a fractional α.

Intuition

Nested integrations fill a triangular region. Swapping integration order turns that region into one power-of-time weighting.

Mathematics

Iⁿf(t) = 1/(n−1)! ∫₀ᵗ (t−s)^(n−1) f(s) ds.
Worked steps
  1. Assume the formula holds for n.
  2. Write I^(n+1)f(t)=∫₀ᵗ Iⁿf(s)ds.
  3. Insert Iⁿf(s)=1/(n−1)! ∫₀ˢ(s−r)^(n−1)f(r)dr.
  4. Swap the order of integration over 0≤r≤s≤t.
  5. The inner integral becomes ∫ᵣᵗ(s−r)^(n−1)ds = (t−r)^n/n.
  6. Combine n(n−1)! = n!.
  7. Therefore I^(n+1)f(t)=1/n! ∫₀ᵗ(t−r)^n f(r)dr.

Trading / modeling consequence

The formula explains why the rough kernel with Γ(α) is a natural continuation of ordinary repeated integration.

Common confusionΓ(n)=(n−1)!, so the Gamma function is the bridge from integer to fractional order.
Memory ruleRepeated integrals collapse to one power-law kernel.
20Rough RiccatiThe λ=0 fractional Riccati equationpp. 8–9

Observation

With a pure power-law kernel, the convolution Riccati equation can be rewritten as a fractional differential equation.

Intuition

Define h to absorb the convolution. The fractional derivative then inverts the fractional integral.

Mathematics

Let h=(κ⋆g)=ν I^αg. Then D^αh=νg. Substitution gives a quadratic fractional Riccati equation in h.
Worked steps
  1. Start from κ(τ)=ντ^(α−1)/Γ(α).
  2. Then (κ⋆g)(τ)=ν I^αg(τ). Define this quantity as h(τ).
  3. Apply D^α: D^αh=νg.
  4. Replace every occurrence of (κ⋆g) in the affine Riccati equation by h.
  5. Replace g by D^αh/ν.
  6. Rearrange to get the fractional Riccati equation.

Trading / modeling consequence

Rough-Heston transforms are solved with fractional rather than ordinary Riccati dynamics.

Common confusionDifferent papers scale h and ν differently; keep one convention consistent before comparing formulas.
Memory rulePower-law convolution becomes fractional differentiation.
21Mittag-LefflerThe fractional analogue of exponential mean reversionp. 9

Observation

With mean reversion, the rough-Heston kernel becomes a Mittag-Leffler kernel.

Intuition

The exponential solves ordinary first-order relaxation; Mittag-Leffler plays an analogous role for fractional relaxation.

Mathematics

κ(τ)=ν τ^(α−1) E(α,α)(−λτ^α), where E(α,β)(z)=Σ(k≥0) z^k / Γ(αk+β).

Trading / modeling consequence

This kernel smoothly connects rough power-law memory with the classical exponential limit.

Common confusionMittag-Leffler is a special function family, not a probability distribution.
Memory ruleFractional mean reversion uses Mittag-Leffler instead of exp.
22Laplace transformsWhy Laplace space simplifies memory equationspp. 9–10

Observation

Convolutions become products under the Laplace transform, turning integral equations into algebra.

Intuition

Laplace space is the natural transform domain for causal lag kernels.

Mathematics

Laplace{f⋆g}(p)=f̂(p)ĝ(p); κ̂₀(p)=ν/p^α; κ̂(p)=ν/(p^α+λ).
Worked steps
  1. Take the Laplace transform of the convolution equation.
  2. Replace each convolution by a product.
  3. Solve the resulting algebraic equation for κ̂(p).
  4. Invert the transform to recover the time-domain Mittag-Leffler kernel.

Trading / modeling consequence

This is the cleanest route to the mean-reverting rough kernel and its resolvent identity.

Common confusionThe Laplace variable p is a transform variable for lag, not a stock price.
Memory ruleConvolution in time = multiplication in Laplace space.
23ResolventDeriving the resolvent identityp. 10

Observation

The Mittag-Leffler kernel is the resolvent of the pure power-law kernel once mean reversion is introduced.

Intuition

The resolvent is the feedback-adjusted memory kernel after repeatedly accounting for the drift interaction.

Mathematics

κ₀ − κ = λ′(κ₀⋆κ), where λ′=λ/ν.
Worked steps
  1. Start in Laplace space with κ̂ = κ̂₀ / [1 + λ′κ̂₀].
  2. Multiply both sides by 1 + λ′κ̂₀.
  3. Obtain κ̂ + λ′κ̂₀κ̂ = κ̂₀.
  4. Rearrange: κ̂₀ − κ̂ = λ′κ̂₀κ̂.
  5. Apply the inverse Laplace transform.
  6. The product κ̂₀κ̂ becomes the convolution κ₀⋆κ.
  7. This yields κ₀−κ = λ′κ₀⋆κ.

Trading / modeling consequence

The identity lets the mean-reverting rough-Heston model retain the affine forward-variance form.

Common confusionA resolvent here is an exact kernel identity, not merely a numerical approximation.
Memory ruleFeedback-adjusted kernel = resolvent.
24Rough HestonForward variance with mean reversionpp. 10–11

Observation

After mean reversion is resolved, the forward-variance shock still has affine kernel form, now using the Mittag-Leffler kernel.

Intuition

Mean reversion changes the memory shape without destroying affine tractability.

Mathematics

dξₜ(u)=κ(u−t)√VₜdWₜ, with κ(τ)=ντ^(α−1)E(α,α)(−λτ^α).

Trading / modeling consequence

This proves that rough Heston with λ>0 remains inside the affine forward-variance family.

Common confusionBounded-variation drift terms disappear from the martingale dynamics of ξ; they do not disappear from the original variance equation.
Memory ruleMean reversion reshapes κ; affine form survives.

The fractional model collapses to classical Heston at H=1/2, so α=1.

Intuition

At α=1, the fractional derivative becomes the ordinary first derivative and the Mittag-Leffler kernel becomes exponential.

Mathematics

α=1 ⇒ H=1/2; E(1,1)(−λτ)=e^(−λτ); D^α becomes d/dτ.

26Classical RiccatiFactoring the Heston Riccati ODEp. 12

Observation

In classical Heston, the convolution equation becomes an ordinary quadratic Riccati ODE.

Intuition

A quadratic polynomial can be expressed through its two roots, which makes the ODE separable.

Mathematics

∂τh = ½η²(h−r+)(h−r−), with h(0)=0.
Worked steps
  1. Write the quadratic Riccati right-hand side in standard polynomial form.
  2. Solve the quadratic equation to obtain roots r+ and r−.
  3. Factor the polynomial as ½η²(h−r+)(h−r−).
  4. Now the variables h and τ can be separated.

Trading / modeling consequence

The root representation is the starting point for the closed-form Heston transform.

Common confusionThe roots depend on the transform argument as well as model parameters; they are not fixed constants across Fourier frequencies.
Memory ruleQuadratic Riccati → factor by roots.
27Classical RiccatiSolving the Heston Riccati ODE line by linep. 12

Observation

The factored Heston Riccati equation has a rational-exponential closed form.

Intuition

Partial fractions convert the nonlinear ODE into a difference of logarithms.

Mathematics

h(τ) = r−[1−e^(−dτ)] / [1−(r−/r+)e^(−dτ)], with d = ½η²(r+−r−).
Worked steps
  1. Separate variables: dh/[(h−r+)(h−r−)] = ½η² dτ.
  2. Use partial fractions: 1/[(h−r+)(h−r−)] = [1/(r+−r−)]{1/(h−r+) − 1/(h−r−)}.
  3. Integrate both sides.
  4. The left side becomes a logarithm of the ratio (h−r+)/(h−r−).
  5. Exponentiate the resulting equation.
  6. Use h(0)=0 to determine the integration constant.
  7. Define d=½η²(r+−r−) and solve algebraically for h.
  8. The result is the displayed rational-exponential formula.

Trading / modeling consequence

This closed form makes classical Heston option pricing computationally efficient.

Common confusionDo not memorize the final fraction before understanding the factorization, partial fractions, and boundary condition.
Memory ruleFactor → partial fractions → logs → boundary condition.
28Characteristic functionAssembling the Heston C and D functionspp. 12–13

Observation

The affine transform separates into a loading on current variance and a loading on long-run variance.

Intuition

Because the forward-variance curve is exponential, integrating g against it splits naturally into current-state and long-run-mean pieces.

Mathematics

φₜ(T;a)=Eₜ[e^(iaX_T)] = exp{iaXₜ + C(a,τ)V̄ + D(a,τ)Vₜ}.
Worked steps
  1. Start from the affine forward-variance transform integral.
  2. Insert ξₜ(s)=V̄+(Vₜ−V̄)e^(−λ(s−t)).
  3. The exponential-weighted integral is the h/D term.
  4. The remaining long-run-mean contribution integrates into C.
  5. Collect coefficients multiplying Vₜ and V̄.

Trading / modeling consequence

The C-D representation is the standard computational form used in Heston Fourier pricing.

Common confusionC and D are deterministic transform functions, not additional stochastic factors.
Memory ruleHeston CF = exp(i a X + C V̄ + D V).
29Information equivalenceFrom characteristic function to density and option pricespp. 13–14

Observation

At one expiry, European option prices, the risk-neutral density, implied volatilities, and the characteristic function encode the same terminal distributional information under regularity conditions.

Intuition

They are different mathematical representations of the same risk-neutral law of the terminal underlying.

Mathematics

φ(a)=E[e^(iaX_T)]; density p(y)=1/(2π)∫ e^(−iay)φ(a) da.
Worked steps
  1. Use Fourier inversion to recover the density from φ.
  2. Integrate option payoffs against that density to obtain European option prices.
  3. Invert Black-Scholes price at each strike to obtain implied volatility.
  4. Conversely, a smooth complete option-price surface gives density by strike differentiation and therefore its characteristic function by Fourier transform.

Trading / modeling consequence

Transform methods can price a whole strike grid efficiently and are central to calibration.

Common confusionEquivalent information does not mean equivalent numerical stability; each representation has different interpolation and estimation issues.

30Empirical diagnosticsVariance memory and rough versus classical Hestonpp. 32–45

Observation

The affine kernel yields explicit variance covariance formulas. Classical Heston implies exponential memory; rough Heston produces scale-rich dependence and fits the empirical variance ACF better in the lecture experiment.

Intuition

Two future variances co-move because both inherit overlapping exposures to the same earlier variance shocks. The kernel controls that overlap.

Mathematics

Covₜ(V_T,V_(T+Δ)) = ∫ₜᵀ ξₜ(s)κ(T−s)κ(T+Δ−s)ds. Classical Heston gives roughly exp(−λΔ); rough Heston gives Varₜ(V_T) proportional to τ^(2H) at short scales.
Worked steps
  1. For classical Heston, insert κ(x)=ηe^(−λx).
  2. The product of the two kernels contributes e^(−λΔ)e^(−2λ(T−s)).
  3. After normalization by Var(V_T), the Δ-dependence is e^(−λΔ).
  4. For rough Heston with λ=0 and flat forward variance, insert κ(x) proportional to x^(α−1).
  5. Variance integrates κ² over the horizon: ∫₀^τ u^(2α−2)du.
  6. Because α=H+1/2, the exponent after integration is 2α−1=2H.
  7. Thus Var(V_T) scales like τ^(2H), matching the rough scaling studied in Lecture 2.

Trading / modeling consequence

The lecture's rough-Heston ACF fit gives H around 0.16, close to the earlier roughness estimate, while smile experiments show that increasing H flattens short-dated smiles.

Common confusionAutocorrelation ρV(Δ) is not the same object as the Brownian spot/variance correlation parameter ρ.
Memory ruleKernel overlap determines memory; H links time-series roughness to option-smile behavior.
